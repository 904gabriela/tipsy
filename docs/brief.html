Briefing one

Why every app you tried “lacks memory”

An AI model remembers nothing between messages. Every time you press send, the app rebuilds one big block of text and ships it. Whatever is in that block, the character knows. Whatever is not, it has genuinely never seen. So “memory” is only ever a question of how the app decides what goes in the block. There are five ways to do it, and the apps you tried each use two or three.

01

The scrollback

Tipsy Chat, and nothing else

Paste the last N messages word for word. Perfect recall of recent scenes, and nothing beats it there. But older messages fall off the end silently, and you re‑send the whole window every single message, so it is the most expensive kind of memory there is. Tipsy's memory setting is literally a size slider for this, and its gem price is charged per 10,000 tokens of it. There is nothing behind the window.

02

The recap

FictionLab's Memory Matrix

Every so often, ask a model to write down what happened, and keep the note instead of the messages. Cheap plot continuity over thousands of messages. But compression eats the specifics: “he promised to bring her the letter by Friday” becomes “they discussed the letter”. FictionLab writes one card per 30 messages, caps you at 100 cards free or 500 paid, and deletes the oldest first unless you pin it. In a 3,000‑message slow burn, your opening arc is gone or reduced to one 500‑character card.

03

The search

SillyTavern, off by default

Store every old message, and when you send a new one, pull back the handful that look most similar. Unlimited cheap archive, great for “what was the whiskey called”. Useless for slow burn: it only fires when your message already resembles the memory, so a character can never bring something up unprompted. SillyTavern's own docs say it “does not guarantee improved memory”.

04

The character sheet

None of the four apps you tried

A live fact sheet kept up to date in the background: what day it is, where everyone is, what they are wearing, who is hurt, what each character wants, how they feel about each other, and every promise still unkept. Always included, never summarised away. This is the one thing that makes a character raise something from 400 messages ago at the right moment. Talemate and a couple of SillyTavern extensions do it. This is your gap.

05

The encyclopedia

Your lorebooks

Entries with trigger words. Say “the Northern Keep” and that entry gets pasted in. Exact, cheap, and entirely under your control, which is why you write them. Blind to anything you paraphrase, and it needs tending. The clever trick the best setups use: save each scene's recap as a lorebook entry with auto‑generated keywords, so old scenes come back only when they are relevant.

The short version. Tipsy gives you one mechanism. FictionLab gives you two and a half and will not take your lorebooks. Nobody gives you the fourth one, which is the one that makes slow burn work. Stacking all five is the whole design.

On your lorebooks. If they came from Chub, Janitor or SillyTavern, they are one of two shapes of the same de facto standard: a .json world‑info file, or a book tucked inside a character card, which is a .png image with the text hidden in it. Both are plain JavaScript to read, including the trigger words, the always‑on entries, the priority order and the timing rules. Whatever you have, we can import it and, just as important, export it back out unchanged. Nothing you own gets trapped in this app.
